## Three ducks and a way out

The player starts in an elevator with the codes of three target containers. Search the warehouse, find a rubber duck in each one, and bring all three to the collection box in the elevator. Delivering the last duck ends the game successfully.

A successful run takes roughly ten minutes. Each new run starts fresh, without persistent saves. Ammunition and flashlight batteries can be found along the way, while the enemy threat grows with time and progress.

## Reading the warehouse

The interface gives container codes rather than a route. The player has to locate the right containers and search their dark interiors. Generation varies which containers are present, their contents, and the duck locations; it always provides the three ducks needed to win.

## Giving the clutter a skeleton

The enemy assembly system is the part I am most proud of. I defined a skeleton with Bézier curves and distributed attachment points along them. Each prop has parameters describing which body regions it suits, influencing the probability of where it will attach.

Once assembled, the creature uses animation clips made in Unity. The clips do not adapt to each individual prop or body shape. The two prototype recordings show early working assembly algorithms in a test scene.

<Video index={2} group open />

## Keeping the assembly affordable

The first approach pulled the attached objects toward their targets every frame. Physics and searching the surroundings for props made the enemy the most expensive part of the game. I moved to Joint connections and refined the algorithms.

In the release, the creature spawns suitable objects around itself and assembles them. It looks as though nearby clutter is coming to life, but it does not take the existing props from the level. I also hid container contents when they were not visible or created them only as the player approached.

<Video index={3} group open />

## Objects that can be shot away

A hit damages both the creature and the struck object. When the object loses its health, it falls off; when the creature loses its health, the whole body collapses. It cannot regenerate. This gave the assembled body a direct role in combat.

The Desert Eagle delivers a precise, powerful hit; the shotgun spreads weaker individual pellets. The enemy targets the player from spawn and moves using Unity NavMesh. Its appearance varies, while health and speed depend mainly on the difficulty reached during the run.

## Tension in an almost empty space

Dim ceiling lights leave the containers difficult to search without a flashlight. Fog, sound, and the restrained PS1-inspired look support the feeling of an empty, quiet place where you cannot be sure you are alone. I mixed CC0 sounds from Freesound for the atmosphere.

The red bar is health and the green bar is running stamina. Escaping a creature can exhaust the player, while searching takes time. The release keeps its settings small: sound volume and mouse sensitivity.

## A smaller idea, brought to release

The first ideas and prototypes date to May–June 2026. The main development push ran from 26 August to the release on 9 September: about two weeks of bringing an existing foundation to a finished game.

The original plan involved procedural hotel floors, multiplayer, and interactions between different weapons and materials. I reduced that scope to one short single-player objective. The final stretch went into polishing, fixing small defects, and testing: a friend checked the game thoroughly while I tried combinations intended to break it.

<Video index={4} group open />

## Independent code and a finished result

I wrote the gameplay code myself to test whether I could still finish a game independently. AI assistance was limited to small routine automations. The models and animations were created manually; some modelling references were AI-generated. The release poster used AI-assisted image processing and Photoshop.

Misform was released for Windows as version 0.1.0, its only published version. The source code remains closed. The most useful result was learning that I could finish and release a game in less than a month, rather than keep expanding it for another year.
