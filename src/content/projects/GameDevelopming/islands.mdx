## A survival sandbox in the sky

IsLands is a block-based game about surviving on floating islands, building, and exploring. There is no fixed ending: you can stay on the first island or bridge the gaps in search of new biomes and resources. Development has ended; the final public build is the playable alpha 0.5.4a.

<Video index={1} group open />

## A world of floating islands

The generation, biomes, and richness of the islands are the parts I am proudest of. Forests, deserts, taiga, and frozen islands introduce different vegetation, creatures, blocks, ores, and structures. Flat sprites sit inside a fully three-dimensional block world.

Blocks made the world easier to manipulate and generate. Sprites suited the visual style and let me draw and import the many trees, characters, and creatures without modelling each one in 3D.

<Image index={9} group open />

## From a sphere to a hidden world map

The first island was a deformed sphere divided into block layers. I expanded that into several spheres, tried three-dimensional noise distorted by other noise, and eventually returned to sphere-based islands guided by a hidden pixel map. Each revision addressed control over shape, intersections, or biome mixing.

One pixel represented one island. Its colour selected the biome, brightness defined size, and position on the map set its world coordinates. Satellite islands were placed around a central biome island, with the parameters kept in a configurable database.

<Gallery indices={[53, 52]} group open />

## Making the same seed mean the same world

Early versions did not pass the seed into every calculation and still used unseeded random values. Small differences accumulated. I tied randomness to deterministic values and used seeded FastNoiseLite noise wherever possible, while continuing to refine island intersections and biome transitions.

<Gallery indices={[21, 48]} group open />

## Keeping generation playable

Generating complex noise and terrain while the player moved was the recurring technical challenge. I worked with parallel processing, partial GPU generation through compute shaders, instancing, and distributing work across frames. Grass and some structures used Graphics.DrawMeshInstanced; trees remained lightweight prefabs.

I deliberately accepted a little more waiting at the start when it made play smoother afterwards. The tables are historical development notes from my i5-9300H, GTX 1660 Ti, 16 GB RAM laptop on Windows 10. Some stages were approximate; I do not have a controlled benchmark of the final build, which can still stutter.

<Image index={49} group open />

## Changing the mesh without breaking interaction

Greedy meshing was implemented almost entirely with AI assistance. The difficult part was adapting UVs, block selection, mining, and placement that had depended on the previous mesh layout. We worked through those dependencies step by step.

Editing a block still rebuilt the affected chunk and its 3 × 3 neighbourhood, including geometry and saved data. I never completed vertical chunk subdivision. LOD experiments also added rebuilding costs without enough benefit to keep them.

<Image index={50} group open />

## From fragments to iron

The first tools start with grass, sticks, and stones. Sticks and stones come from forest-floor blocks or loose resources on the ground. Stone tools open up wood, stone, and clay; a campfire or clay furnace leads into iron smelting, better tools, and the armour table.

Materials contribute different properties to armour, combined with their quantities and the rest of the recipe. Iron, for example, provides strength but adds weight. Inventory improvements such as stack splitting, quick transfers, and recipe variants made this larger loop more convenient.

<Gallery indices={[36, 3]} group open />

## Weather that changes everyday decisions

Rain can extinguish a campfire. In a frozen biome, the campfire and torch provide warmth. Wood, amber, coal, and other materials serve as furnace fuel. Farming follows a simple loop: find wild potatoes, till the ground with a shovel, plant, wait, and harvest.

<Gallery indices={[24, 15]} group open />

## Teaching creatures to understand blocks

Early NPCs caught their colliders on corners and sometimes walked off islands. The revised system scanned the surroundings into a virtual spatial representation and recalculated it when the environment changed. Routes could be evaluated by speed and danger rather than simply pushing toward a target.

The world grew from rabbits and regional variants into a broader population of animals and hostile creatures. Reworking their movement was also an optimisation task, not just a way to add new behaviours.

<Gallery indices={[29, 34]} group open />

## Astra Editor · make the world your own

Astra Editor lets players name a texture pack, paint it, save it, and use or share it. Original atlases can also be exported for editing elsewhere and imported again. The recording shows an edited ground texture becoming visible in the game.

The .aetp file stores serialised JSON with pixel edits relative to the original pack; ZIP import supports the external-editing workflow. Delivered customisation focused on current blocks and items, nicknames, and music controls. Players also sent me around three to five of their own texture packs.

## A world shared with players

Multiplayer became possible with Mirror and substantial AI assistance. The host held and processed world state, synchronising it with clients. A joining player received a save snapshot from the host and loaded that current world. Inventory desynchronisation and phantom items were among the problems I worked through.

I hosted sessions through Radmin with roughly six people playing together. The screenshots preserve shared playtests and player-created builds.

World saves included edited blocks, nearby entities and their health, the player state, time of day, positions, and velocities. That snapshot also formed the starting point for a joining client.

## Menus and controls made for the game

Each main-menu scene was assembled separately to demonstrate the current version. A gently mouse-driven camera revealed its depth; some versions included rabbits with simplified AI or precipitation particles. Community requests also led to gamepad support with a virtual cursor and action bindings.

The final build has Russian and English localisation. I also added an in-game guide after repeated player questions about progression and controls.

## Music that follows the island

The music manager selected tracks for the current biome, weather, time of day, and configured mood. I composed Adventure, Nixell, Lazy Algorithm, and Warm Horizon; INNA1 also contributed music to the game. This let the soundtrack respond to the surroundings.

<Image index={7} group open />

## One developer, a detailed plan, and AI assistance

By the end I was the sole developer, with INNA1 contributing music. I used detailed Obsidian task lists and canvases to keep releases organised, following planned tasks in order rather than continuously expanding the update.

Implementation relied substantially on AI, including greedy meshing and networking. I tested new behaviour by running it in Unity and reviewed code at a high level. I can no longer reliably separate every hand-written section from generated code. The textures were drawn by me using image references; release illustrations used AI generation and Photoshop editing.

## Published and played

I released Windows and Linux builds and published the game on itch.io. Linux required resolving rendering and shader incompatibilities through build settings. The supplied itch.io snapshot records 651 downloads from 11 November 2025 to 10 November 2026.

<Image index={76} group open />

## Bringing the game to Microsoft Store

Microsoft Store meant repeatedly converting the ordinary executable-and-folders build into its required package and adapting save locations to AppData. Its supplied analytics snapshot records 357 installs. Downloads and installs are separate platform metrics, not a count of unique players.

<Image index={77} group open />

## What I would take into the next project

The original idea centred on a home base and expeditions to bring resources back. In practice, carrying everything in the inventory was easier, and building mostly became a way to bridge islands. That gap between the intended loop and actual play became an important design lesson.

As updates grew, I shifted toward polishing each feature more carefully. My main lesson was to choose a smaller scope and define a clearer endpoint and schedule. IsLands remains an archived alpha and a record of that experience.
